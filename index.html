Camera Movement & Cinematography

Nine Basic Camera Movements (7 camera + 2 lens moves), industry-standard framing terminology, cinematic composition rules, technical controls, and the three phases of video production.

Basic Camera Movements

Of course, there are more types of moves and complicated combinations, but mastery begins with these nine fundamental operations (seven camera moves + two lens adjustments):

1. Pan

Panning is when you move your camera horizontally; either left to right or right to left, while its base is fixated on a certain point. You are not moving the position of the camera itself, just the direction it faces. These types of shots are great for establishing a sense of location within your story.

Why: To follow a subject or show the distance between two objects. Pan shots also work great for panoramic views such as a shot from a mountaintop to the valley below.

Rule Always start on a still shot, begin the pan, and finish on a still shot. Practice first. Look at the scene as the pan reaches the middle portion between the beginning and end of the scene. If there is nothing worth seeing, then the pan isn't worth shooting.

2. Tilt

Moving the camera’s lens up or down while keeping its horizontal axis constant. Nod your head up and down — this is tilting.

Why: Like panning, to follow a subject or to show the top and bottom of a stationary object. With a tilt, you can also show how high something is. For example, a slow tilt up a Giant Sequoia tree shows its grandness and enormity.

3. Dolly

A dolly is when you move the entire camera forwards and backwards, typically on some sort of track or motorized vehicle. This type of movement can create beautiful, flowing effects when done correctly. If you want to attempt a dolly, make sure your track is stable and will allow for fluid movement.

Why: To follow an object smoothly to get a unique perspective, usually as either point of view (POV) of the character, following the character, or tracking in front of the character looking back at them to show them moving through space.

Note: Not to be confused with the piece of equipment called A Dolly, used to create movement in either a Dolly or Tracking shot.

4. Truck (Tracking)

Trucking is the same as dollying, only you are moving the camera laterally from left to right instead of in and out.

Why: To show the character move across a space, maintain pace alongside walking characters, or reveal architectural environments dynamically.

5. Pedestal & Crane / Boom

Pedestal is primarily used in studio work, where the hydraulic camera mount can be pulled straight up or pushed down to adjust the vertical height of the camera while shooting.

Crane or Boom: Refers to using either a mechanical jib arm / boom (pictured above) or a large motorized crane. This works and looks similar to a construction crane. It is used for high sweeping shots or to follow the action of your subject.

Why: Gives a bird's-eye view. It looks as if the camera is swooping down from above. Movie directors use this for street scenes so they can shoot from above the crowd and the traffic, and then move smoothly down to eye level.

One very good crane shot demo

6. Stabilized Shot

A Stabilized shot uses mechanical or motorized counterbalances to eliminate the shaky handheld look. The image shows a professional Steadicam harness, but this term describes many forms of stabilization from 3-axis motorized gimbals and sliders to camera drones.

Why: To follow an object through twists and turns. Although the dolly is great, its movements are limited to flat tracks. With a stabilizer, you can follow someone seamlessly through tight hallways, doors, and around rooms.

Iconic Example: Goodfellas

The Famous Long Take: Goodfellas (Copacabana)

7. Handheld — "Shaky Cam"

This does not simply mean the camera was handheld. Rather, it means the shot looks intentionally and obviously handheld.

Why: To introduce a feeling of unease, excitement, documentary realism, and uncertainty. In horror or action movies, directors use handheld shots when something dangerous is about to happen, deliberately making viewers feel unstable.

"Handheld" is also used as a production note regarding how a shot will be captured. Due to the spontaneity of the action, news and documentary crews rely on shoulder-mounted cameras to balance the weight and minimize high-frequency shake.

Essential Tip When shooting handheld, do not zoom in! Keep it wide. The more you zoom in, the more violently pronounced any shaking appears. Move physically closer to your subject and shoot at the widest focal length possible. Handheld is best suited for moving action; it looks amateurish when shooting static landscapes or architecture.

Lens Movements (In-Camera Optical Adjustments)

8. Zoom

Changing the focal length of the camera lens, usually from wide-angle to telephoto or vice-versa. Zooming in uses the telephoto portion of the lens, bringing objects visually closer, compressing background distance, and narrowing the field of view. Zooming out expands the field of view into a wide-angle shot, accentuating depth perception and spatial distance.

Why: To bring distant objects closer without moving the camera, or to emphasize scale and perspective.

Rule Continuous zooming in and out is annoying to viewers. Don't zoom while shooting unless the dramatic beat calls for it. Always use a tripod when zooming. Start on a still shot, zoom smoothly, and hold your final frame still.

9. Rack Focus

Focusing on one object, like an actor's face, while leaving everything behind them blurred. Then adjusting the focus ring so the foreground actor blurs while a background person or object snaps sharp.

Why: It creates an in-camera transition similar to an edit by constructing two distinct compositions within a single continuous shot. Widely utilized in dramas and thrillers to transfer emotional attention between characters during tense dialogue.

Tip Always use a tripod and rehearse the focus mark beforehand. A rack focus looks jarring if the camera shakes during the pull.

Camera Shot Types & Framing

Descriptions of shot composition carry precise cinematic definitions. Everyone on a film and video crew must share this universal shorthand to work efficiently and minimize confusion.

Shots Based on Subject Size in Frame

Extreme Long / Wide Shot (ELS / EWS)

A panoramic view of an exterior location photographed from a considerable distance, often up to a quarter-mile away. Characters are tiny elements deep in the background. The surroundings dominate the frame, especially in high-angle vistas.

As the camera moves further away, the visual field demands a more complex read from the spectator to decode the relationship between human subjects and the massive landscape.

Long / Wide Shot (LS / WS)

The subject or characters are visible in full from head to toe within their surrounding environment. While "Wide Shot" is often used synonymously, "Wide" technically refers to optical field of view (wide-angle lens), whereas a "Long Shot" can be captured using a telephoto lens from a great physical distance.

Iconic Example: Braveheart

Full Shot (FS)

Shows the human figure in its entirety from head to toe, leaving comfortable but minimal space above the head and below the feet.

Medium Long Shot (MLS)

Frames the character from the waist, hips, or knees up (also called a "Cowboy Shot" if framed to reveal holstered revolvers). Subjects typically occupy half to two-thirds of the frame height.

Medium Shot (MS)

Frames the figure from the waist or stomach up. The "bread and butter" of videography and television, providing personal engagement without feeling uncomfortably invasive.

How to Frame an MS Like a Master

Medium Close-Up (MCU)

Frames the chest/shoulders and head of one, two, or three characters. Multi-character MCUs visually signify intimacy, shared intent, or solidarity.

Close-Up (CU)

Shows the full head and collar line to reveal raw actor emotion.

A Clockwork Orange (1971)

Extreme Close-Up (ECU)

Isolates specific details — eyes, mouth, or a watch face — demanding focused thematic scrutiny.

Citizen Kane (1941)

Two Shot / Three Shot

Frames two actors interacting in medium/long perspective. (If framed on a single actor, the shorthand term is Single).

Pulp Fiction (1994)

Establishing Shot

Placed at the head of a scene to establish geographic context and mood.

Seinfeld (Tom's Restaurant)

Master Shot (Cover Shot)

A continuous take of an entire scene from start to finish from a vantage point that captures all character geography and action. In post-production, the editor cuts in closer angles and inserts, but can always cut back to the master shot to cover edits.

Iconic Example: Taxi Driver (1976)

Over-the-Shoulder Shot (OTS)

Positioned behind one character's shoulder looking at the face of the speaking actor. The foreground actor's shoulder establishes depth and grounds the viewer directly inside the conversation. In major film productions, body doubles or stand-ins often sit for the off-camera actor during OTS coverage.

Masterclass: Heat (1995) Restaurant Scene

Robert De Niro & Al Pacino face off in a restaurant. Notice how the OTS framing tightens from medium shots into suffocating extreme close-ups as emotional tension escalates.

Watch De Niro vs. Pacino in Heat (1995)

Camera Angles & Emotional Psychology

Low Angle

Camera sits below the subject, looking up. Makes characters appear powerful, dominant, imposing, or physically threatening.

Inglourious Basterds (2009)

Brad Pitt (Aldo Raine) and B.J. Novak looking straight down at carved foreheads.

High Angle

Films from above looking down on the subject. Aims to portray the subject as vulnerable, submissive, or overwhelmed.

Matilda (1996)

Dutch Angle (Canted)

The camera is tilted on its roll axis so the horizon line is diagonal. Symbolizes psychological madness, disorientation, drugs, or acute danger.

12 Monkeys (1995)

Overhead (Bird's Eye)

Mounted 90 degrees straight down via drone, crane, or ceiling truss. Provides a detached god-like observation of geometry and geography.

The Avengers (2012)

Cinematic Composition Rules

The Rule of Thirds & The Golden Ratio

In cinematography and photography, two horizontal and two vertical imaginary lines divide the frame into nine equal segments. In classical art, this visual principle originated from the Golden Ratio. Placing focal subjects along these gridlines or at their intersections produces inherently more engaging and natural compositions than dead-centering everything.

Intersecting third lines guide viewer eye attention.

Horizons aligned on upper or lower third.

Subject's eyes placed exactly at intersection points.

The 180-Degree Rule (The Line of Action)

The 180-degree rule maintains spatial continuity on screen. Imagine an invisible axis connecting two interacting characters. All camera setups for that scene must remain on one side of that line.

When the camera stays on the correct side of the axis, Character A consistently looks screen-right toward Character B, and Character B looks screen-left toward Character A. Our internal cinematic literacy tells us they are facing one another.

What happens if the director crosses the line? Both characters would suddenly appear to look in the exact same direction, confusing the audience and making it appear as if a second person is standing in the room.

Case Study: Crossing the Line in The Dark Knight

Look Room & Head Room

There are two parts to negative space rules in framing:

Look Room (Nose Room)

If your subject is looking toward screen-left, place their body on the right third of the frame so they have open "room to look" across the composition:

Head Room

Particularly critical for interviews, corporate video, and documentaries. Leave just a small, comfortable amount of space above the head so it is not cropped out. Too much headroom pushes the face down and creates an empty, amateur shot.

Camera Operations & Technical Controls

Professional cinematography requires managing every component of the photographic and recording chain:

Lens & Exposure

  • Focus: Sharpness calibration on the primary subject.
  • Zoom: Optical focal length adjustment.
  • Aperture (f-stop): Iris opening controlling depth of field and incoming light.
  • Shutter Speed: Exposure time per frame (standard 180-degree rule: 1/50s for 24fps or 1/60s for 30fps).
  • ISO / Gain / Sensitivity: Sensor electronic amplification. High values introduce digital noise/grain.
  • ND Filters (Neutral Density): "Sunglasses for your lens" enabling shallow depth of field in bright sunlight.
  • Color Temperature / White Balance: Calibrating kelvin values (3200K tungsten to 5600K daylight) to ensure clean whites.

Recording & Media

  • Media: High-speed SDXC (V60/V90), CFast 2.0, or NVMe SSD recording media.
  • Resolution: 1080p Full HD, 4K UHD, or 6K/8K acquisition.
  • Compression Codecs:
    • ProRes 422 / DNxHR (Intra-frame master quality)
    • H.264 / H.265 (HEVC inter-frame delivery)
    • RAW bitstreams (uncompressed sensor data)

Audio & Menu Controls

  • Input Select: Internal onboard mic, 3.5mm mini-jack, or balanced XLR inputs.
  • Auto vs. Manual Levels: Always use Manual levels peaking around -12 dB.
  • Mic Types: Dynamic, shotgun condenser, or wired/wireless lavaliere.
  • Menu Access: Color profiles (Rec.709 vs. Log profiles), zebra stripes, peaking, and zebras.
  • Viewfinder Options: False color exposure overlays and framing guidelines.

The Three Phases of Video Production

Every motion picture or video project progresses sequentially through Preproduction, Production, and Postproduction:

1. Preproduction

All planning events that take place prior to physical shooting:

  • Idea Generation: Brainstorming, original concepts, topics of interest, or client brief.
  • Scriptwriting: The written blueprint of action and dialogue. Can be a detailed shot list if no dialogue exists.
  • Storyboarding: A sequence of drawings with camera direction and notes representing planned shots.
  • Casting: Selecting auditioning actors or interview subjects.
  • Location Scouting:
    • Look & feel of space
    • Associated location fees & permits
    • Natural lighting & window orientation
    • Acoustic noise & logistics
    • Electrical power availability & parking

2. Production

The physical recording of picture and sound on set or on location:

  • Framing & Field of View: Calibrating wide, medium, and tight coverage.
  • Camera Moves: Executing smooth pans, tilts, dollies, and tracking shots.
  • Audio Recording: Positioning boom mics and wiring lavalieres for clean dialogue.
  • Lighting: 3-point lighting setup (Key, Fill, Backlight) using natural and artificial sources.

3. Postproduction

All operations between recording wrap and rendering the finished master:

  • Capturing & Ingesting: Transferring media and organizing footage into bins.
  • Clips & Trimming: Trimming head and tail handles without deleting source files.
  • Building Sequences: Assembling wide, medium, and tight angles along the edit timeline to compress dramatic time.
  • Titles & Graphics: Adding lower-third identifiers and rolling credits.
  • Sound Design & Mixing: Adding room tone, foley, voiceover narration, and musical score.
  • Transitions & Effects: Applying dissolves, color grading, and vintage looks.
  • Exporting: Encoding master and web delivery video formats (.mp4, .mov, .avi).
